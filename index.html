Sleep Quality and Student Performance

A student-led investigation into how stress, depressive symptoms, and electronic device use relate to sleep quality among high school students.

View the data Explore the booth

Research question

How do stress, depressive symptoms, and electronic device use relate to sleep quality?

The project began as a broad curiosity about sleep and school performance, then became a more measurable quantitative study. The strongest learning came from narrowing variables, handling imperfect data, and understanding that real evidence rarely forms a perfect pattern.

Evidence

Learning process

What changed from idea to evidence

01

Refined the question

The team moved from “more sleep means better performance” toward measurable factors: stress, PHQ-9 depressive symptoms, electronic device habits, and sleep timing.

02

Standardized collection

A cluster sample from Meraki House was surveyed anonymously after ethics, consent, and privacy considerations were planned.

03

Analyzed messy data

Scatter and distribution patterns were not perfect. That became a research lesson: weak trends, outliers, and limitations still matter when interpreted honestly.

04

Evaluated reliability

The group identified limits such as a small sample, descriptive analysis only, and a need for broader demographic detail and more standardized measurement tools.

Two-minute takeaway

Sleep quality is not just bedtime.

A student’s sleep is shaped by a system of habits and pressures: device routines, self-expectations, academic stress, mood, and consistency. Good research does not only chase a neat result; it tests whether the evidence is strong enough to support the claim.

Expo booth experience

A visitor can understand the research in under two minutes.

1. Quick prediction

Visitors choose which factor they think most strongly relates to poor sleep: stress, depressive symptoms, or device use.

2. Reveal the evidence

The website shows the late-night pattern and stress distribution, making the gap between expectation and evidence visible.

3. Reflect on method

Visitors compare “claim” versus “evidence” and identify why small samples and descriptive results limit certainty.
